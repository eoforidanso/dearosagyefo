Letter I

On the Matter of Dumsor & the Audacity of Darkness

Dear Osagyefo,

I hope this letter finds you in a dimension that has, at the very minimum, twenty-four hours of uninterrupted electricity. We do not, as you may have surmised, have that here.

You spoke of industrialisation. You built the Akosombo Dam — a colossal monument to ambition and the optimism of a newly independent nation. Then Ghana got creative.

We created something called Dumsor — a portmanteau from Twi meaning "off-on." The lights go off. Then they come on. Then they go off again. Sometimes for hours.

We have adapted remarkably. Generators are standard fixtures. Power banks, candle sellers, generator fuel vendors — an entire ecosystem built around light-off has emerged.

We do not blame you, Osagyefo. You gave us the dam. What we did with the transmission lines afterwards is entirely our own achievement.

Yours faithfully, with a charged power bank,

A Ghanaian with a Melting Fridge

Letter II

On Mobile Money & Accidental Socialism

Dear Osagyefo,

You championed socialism. We laughed at it in the 1980s, then thirty years later accidentally rebuilt parts of it inside our mobile phones.

Mobile Money — MoMo — launched in 2009. It allows anyone with a phone to send, receive, and store money through agents at roadside kiosks, barbershops, and churches.

The redistribution is staggering. A market woman in Tamale can receive payment from her daughter in Accra within seconds. The unbanked were, within a decade, sending money like texts.

You tried to build state infrastructure. The market built its own through the air.

We found your socialism, Kwame. It was yellow, fit in a pocket, and was MTN-branded.

With mobile-transferred regards,

A MoMo Agent in Kumasi

Letter III

On Governance & the Theatre of Democracy

Dear Osagyefo,

You governed for life, Osagyefo. We now hold elections every four years, which sounds democratic until you realize it's the same people in different suits.

We have a Parliament. It's picturesque. The debates are spirited. The outcomes are predetermined. Ministers speak eloquently about accountability while their relatives secure contracts.

You built a one-party state in the name of unity. We built a two-party system in the name of choice. Both achieved the same result: Ghanaians who know exactly what's happening but have perfected the art of the resigned sigh.

Democracy, as practiced here, is performance art. And we are a captive, weary audience.

Yours in democratic disillusionment,

A Political Observer from Accra

Letter IV

On Pan-Africanism & Instagram Filters

Dear Osagyefo,

You envisioned a united Africa. We now celebrate Africa Day with Instagram stories featuring kente cloth filters and hashtags that vanish in 24 hours.

Your Organization of African Unity was built on policy papers and summit meetings. Our version is built on "Detty December" and viral dance challenges.

We wear dashikis to Afrobeats concerts. We quote Fanon in Twitter threads. We celebrate African excellence — but only if it trends.

You wanted political unity. We achieved aesthetic unity. Progress, perhaps, just not the kind you imagined.

Yours in curated solidarity,

A Digital Native with Pan-African Captions

Letter V

On the Matter of "Going Abroad"

Dear Osagyefo,

You studied in America and Britain, then returned to build Ghana. We study in America and Britain, then stay to become Uber drivers with PhDs.

"Going abroad" has become Ghana's most successful export industry. We send our best. They send back remittances and nostalgic Instagram posts about jollof rice.

The diaspora speaks passionately about Ghana's potential from three time zones away. They know the solution to all our problems. They just can't implement them from their studio apartments in Atlanta.

You called for Africans to return home. We return for Christmas, complain about the heat, and leave before New Year.

Yours from Terminal 3 Heathrow,

An Economist Working as a Care Assistant

Letter VI

On National Pride & Football Victories

Dear Osagyefo,

We united as a nation exactly twice since independence: When we won AFCON in 1963, and when we beat Nigeria 2-0 in World Cup qualifiers.

For 90 minutes, Ghana is One. Northerners and Southerners, Akans and Ewes, PhDs and Okada riders — all screaming at a screen in unified rage when the referee makes a questionable call.

Then the match ends. We return to our regularly scheduled tribalism.

Perhaps you should have built the nation on a football pitch. It's the only place where we consistently remember we're all Ghanaian.

Yours in temporary solidarity,

A Black Stars Supporter (Until We Lose)

Letter VII

On Highlife Music & Cultural Memory

Dear Osagyefo,

You danced to E.T. Mensah in the State House. We now twerk to Afrobeats produced in Lagos and mixed in Atlanta. Progress is subjective.

Highlife — the music of independence, of optimism, of saxophones and hope — now plays mostly at funerals and on "throwback Thursday" playlists.

The youth want bass drops and American slang. Cultural preservation is for museum directors and ethnomusicology students writing theses no one will read.

We've traded our musical heritage for streaming numbers. E.T. Mensah has 50,000 monthly Spotify listeners. Wizkid has 50 million.

Yours to the rhythm of forgotten melodies,

A Music Historian Mourning in B-flat

Letter VIII

On Education & the University Legacy

Dear Osagyefo,

You built universities. We turned them into degree mills where lecturers are gods and students protest with Twitter hashtags instead of placards.

The University of Ghana — your crown jewel — now graduates thousands annually into an economy that needs hundreds. The rest become "hustlers," a polite term for the educated unemployed.

We have the degrees. We have the potential. What we don't have are the jobs, the infrastructure, or the political will to use what we've learned.

Your universities were meant to build the nation. Ours build résumés for foreign job markets.

Yours in educated unemployment,

A Graduate with a 2:1 and No Prospects

Letter IX

On Agriculture & the Rice We Import

Dear Osagyefo,

You championed self-sufficiency. We now import rice from Thailand, tomatoes from Burkina Faso, and somehow still call ourselves the breadbasket.

Ghana — with fertile soil, two rainy seasons, and motivated farmers — cannot feed itself. We have the land. We lack the will, the policy, and apparently, the memory of why food security matters.

The youth flee the farms for Accra, where they sell imported goods they can't afford to buy themselves. Meanwhile, the rice from Asia piles up in our markets, cheap and subsidized by governments that take farming seriously.

We have become importers of the very things we once grew. That is a special kind of failure.

Yours from a field left fallow,

A Farmer's Son Who Moved to the City

Letter X

On Justice & the Speed of Courts

Dear Osagyefo,

You believed in swift justice. Our courts move so slowly that criminals age out of their sentences before trial begins. At least they're patient.

A case from 2010 is still "pending." Files disappear. Witnesses forget. Lawyers postpone. Justice delayed is justice denied, but in Ghana, justice delayed is just... Tuesday.

The wealthy hire lawyers who know how to navigate the system — which means knowing how to freeze it indefinitely. The poor plead guilty to move things along.

You wanted a legal system. We have a legal theater where the performance matters more than the verdict.

Yours in judicial purgatory,

A Lawyer Who's Seen It All and Seen Nothing

Letter XI

The Persistent Challenge of Small-Scale Mining

Dear Osagyefo,

I'm writing to you again about the devastating impact of small-scale surface mining or galamsey, on our water bodies. Despite numerous attempts to curb this menace, including deploying a "heart surgeon" to the forest, the situation has only worsened. Even the water guards those men and women in Adidas tracks commissioned to tackle galamsey have mysteriously gone missing.

Now, Dr. Hannah Bissiw, a seemingly gentle lady, has ventured into the heart of the forest, "Rambo-style," to confront the issue. This move baffles me, especially considering we've previously deployed the military to the same areas with limited success. Yet, Dr. Bissiw, equipped with her own gear, a cameraman and a few policemen somehow successfully arrested several Chinese nationals actively destroying our water bodies.

It's ironic, given that this government, when in opposition, vowed to end galamsey within a week, even promising to repeal the Legislative Instrument to ban galamsey in forest preserves. The last time the Deputy Communication Minister was questioned about declaring a state of emergency on galamsey, she skirted the issue, offering a convoluted and evasive response.

Compounding this, I've heard the Chinese Ambassador question how his citizens gained access to Ghana's forests, seemingly implying complicity from within our own ranks. Given their skilled labor force, it's not surprising the Chinese are involved in these activities. However, the barbaric treatment Dr. Bissiw allegedly meted out to some individuals, seeing denying even a basic request for water, is deeply concerning and strikes at the core of human dignity.

This incident brings to mind the Hawthorne effect: when cameras are rolling, people tend to act differently in her case acting like she's tougher than a brick. Dr. Bissiw's intentions were noble, no doubt, but her "jeans and a dream" approach to galamsey feels a bit like bringing a butter knife to a chainsaw fight. Without a state of emergency or the LI being repealed, her forest trek is sadly destined to be nothing more than a strenuous nature walk with an educational detour to the local kindergarten.

Talk is cheap,

Ato_KD

Letter XII

A Nation's DRS Dramas and Delusions

Dear Osagyefo,

As we tiptoe into 2026, the Republic is humming with a bizarre blend of football ecstasy and economic heartbreak. Yes, Ghana qualified for the World Cup in the USA, Canada, and Mexico but the joy evaporated faster than the Cedi on a Monday morning. FIFA, in its boundless "kindness," expanded the tournament by 16 teams, then immediately balanced that generosity by pricing tickets as though they were selling seats on a private shuttle to Jupiter. Ghanaians are now engaged in a nationwide brainstorming session on how to cross the Atlantic, with some wondering whether selling a kidney qualifies as a "strategic financial instrument." At this point, even the black market is asking us to calm down.

While we were busy calculating airfare with the precision of a WAEC examiner marking scripts at midnight, the world was jolted by the dramatic "importation" of Venezuela's President Maduro, his wife, and their Attorney General to the United States. Before anyone could even whisper "geopolitics," our very own Honorable Ablakwa sprinted to the global stage with an "open statement" on Ghana's position. Osagyefo, the letter was so limp it could have doubled as a bookmark in a children's storybook. There is real danger in poking our noses into high-voltage American legal matters; one misplaced syllable and the US might simply announce, "No visas for Ghanaians," and that will be the end of our Miami pilgrimage.

Someone needs to gently remind the young man of the immortal words of the late Prof. Mills during the Gbagbo saga: Di wo fie asem. We are trying to go and watch football, not audition for a role in a Latin American coup documentary.

But if you thought that was the comedic peak, allow me to introduce Dr. Stephen Amoah. A man with a PhD in Actuarial Science a credential he wears like a bulletproof vest in a neighbourhood where no one is shooting. In his attempt to support his preferred candidate in the NPP primary, he delivered a monologue so chaotic it could qualify as experimental theatre. He proudly declared that he is one of only five people on Earth who have cracked a mysterious financial-economic model from 1964. A model so complex that even the global experts who never asked about it are still baffled. Truly, the world is ungrateful.

This is the same Dr. Amoah who, in 2019, faced questions about procurement breaches involving 1.1 million cedis paid to an audit firm to investigate whether his predecessor had misused 2 million cedis. In essence, he spent 1 million to find 2 million a financial strategy so innovative it deserves its own TED Talk. When the court asked if he should be held responsible, he delivered a "Yes and No" answer that plunged the room into philosophical crisis.

When confronted with the tape, Dr. Amoah claimed "doctoring," while performing some surgical-grade wordplay of his own. For a man who usually has an answer for everything, he suddenly seemed very busy tiptoeing around the facts. It was less an explanation and more a linguistic interpretive dance the kind that makes judges stare into the distance and reconsider their career choices.

A man of such rare brilliance should not be lobbying to be a minister in a country of barely 30 million people. He should be in Washington, marching straight into the IMF headquarters to demand his rightful position as Managing Director. After all, why settle for Ghana's budget when you can supervise the entire planet's?

And in a twist that surprised absolutely no one, word has reached us that the former Finance Minister has been "scouted" by ICE agents in America. It appears my suspicion was correct: the gentleman's travel plans were strictly one-way. And honestly, can we blame him for preferring an American holding cell over a Ghanaian greeting party? After witnessing Chairman Wontumi lose 50 pounds in a record-breaking 24-hour security-service fitness retreat, the Minister clearly decided he wasn't ready for that kind of "body transformation."

So long,

Ato KD

Letter XIII

The Toxicology Report: What's Really in Our Drinks?

Dear Osagyefo,

Word on the street is that the same government that scrapped the betting tax now wants to build a rehab center in the Ashanti Region for the very people who got addicted to betting because there was no tax. Sports betting and online gambling have become deeply woven into the fabric of youth culture in Ghana. Billboards line our streets, mobile apps make betting effortless, and celebrities endorse it with catchy slogans and promise laden jingles.

It's a case of giving you the knife, watching you cut yourself, and then offering you a Band-Aid, with ribbon-cutting and media coverage, of course. Meanwhile, the government has decided to cancel licensure exams for professional teachers. A licensure exam acts as a quality control mechanism, ensuring that those entering the classroom have met a nationally recognized benchmark of competence.

Just as we wouldn’t want an unlicensed doctor treating patients or an uncertified engineer building bridges, we shouldn’t entrust our children’s education to individuals whose knowledge and teaching skills have not been formally assessed. Teaching is not simply about passing on information; it’s about managing classrooms, applying pedagogy, and adapting to diverse learning needs. In other news, GTEC has suddenly woken up and started sending love letters to people parading around as “Doctors” without the papers to back it up.

Enter Hasan Ayariga who, depending on the day, has either three doctorate degrees, two honorary titles, one academic credential. Turns out the university that awarded him his “diploma” in the U. S. Regis University might just be fake. Ask him a simple question, and by the time he’s done answering, you’ll be questioning your own existence. You won’t remember what you asked, why you asked it, or how you ended up in a philosophical fog, somewhere between confusion and a trance-like daze.

The days when our grandfathers brewed herbs in clay pots, filling the air with the healing aroma of neem leaves, are long gone. Back then, one whiff of that bitter steam and malaria would pack its bags. Even catarrh didn’t dare overstay its welcome. We’ve traded mortar and pestle for plastic bottles and powdered promises. Our new healers don’t live in the village shrine they ride trotro and set up shop in traffic.

Before your headache even arrives, they’ve sold you a cure. One magical powder to cure diabetes, piles, waist pain, broken hearts, and even erectile dysfunction talk about a miracle in a sachet. Some of these so-called "medications" are secretly loaded with psychotropics, which explains why people report “deep, restful sleep” not realizing they’ve basically been chemically sedated into dreamland.

What knocks you out at night also wakes up your appetite !! They wait patiently in lorries until the last seat is taken. The bus jolts forward and the sermon begins. For 10 to 15 minutes, you’re treated to a TED Talk on the wonders of a suspiciously unlabelled bottle and powders in sachets. To seal the deal, two “random” passengers rise to testify to its miraculous powers.

Occasionally, the driver chimes in with a confident nod, because if your trotro driver believes it, it must be true.

Doctors everywhere,
Ato_KD
Letter XIV

Speeches Are Paper, Change Is Concrete

Dear Osagyefo,

Your last presence at the United Nations General Assembly in 1961 remains a touchstone of African leadership. This year, the current President of the Republic stood on the same global stage in New York, albeit dressed in a colorful smock, to deliver his address to the world.

During your time, following the assassination of Patrice Lumumba after his appeal to the UN, you articulated the profound hope that the United Nations represented for world peace. Similarly, President Mahama delivered a powerful address, demanding greater representation for Africa within the UN Security Council structure and highlighting the fundamental injustices that contravene the organization's founding principles.

However, the history of this great hall is filled with beautiful speeches that earned standing ovations, from Madiba to the Otumfuo's keynote address six years ago, and, of course, Barack Obama. Again, history tells us anything, it's that the UN stage is a hall of beautiful, perishable rhetoric. These examples underscore a crucial truth: Words are fleeting and easily forgotten. People are ultimately remembered not by the eloquence of their speeches, but by the tangible changes they effect for humanity. Speeches are paper, change is concrete!

Here at home, President Mahama proved he knows the Constitution of Ghana inside and out, using its delicate clauses to expertly arrange the departure of a Chief Justice. Yet, shortly thereafter, he took to the UN General Assembly to champion a cause that seemed to forget the very nature of established power. He was there, passionately demanding reform and railing against the veto power enshrined in Article 27 of the UN Charter that beautiful little rule that lets any P5 member block a substantive resolution and ensures nothing truly binding happens against their core interests.

President Mahama once vowed to decisively tackle the destructive illegal mining known as "galamsey." Regrettably, his administration's approach has been arguably the worst in Ghana's history. It appears the fight against this menace has been abandoned, choosing instead to focus on benefiting from the gold proceeds rather than ending the practice that is destroying our water bodies like never before.

The landmark repeal of the criminal libel law under Kufuor is being aggressively undermined currently. Politicians, it seems, haven't learned the lessons of history and are rushing to embrace mechanisms that will ultimately silence them. The chilling warning holds true: "Freedom after the speech won't be guaranteed." All those beautiful speeches are wonderful, but you can't clean up a river with a standing ovation. Back home, your water is being destroyed like never before.

So much to do, so little done,

Ato_KD

Letter XV

Ankwanoma OSP: The Singing Prosecutor and the Bailed-Out Lawyer

Dear Osagyefo,

You'll be amused to know that the theater of the absurd in our Republic has a new leading man: Martin Kpebu. It appears the man who once championed the cause of the downtrodden is now on a singular mission to perform a final magic trick, trying to make the Office of the Special Prosecutor (OSP) disappear entirely.

I remember when Martin first came onto the scene through Joy Newsfile. Back then, he was a very unassuming, learned lawyer, a shy gentleman who seemed to know the Constitution like the palm of his hand. He spoke with the quiet precision of a surgeon. But oh, how times change! The moment he started wearing "The Hat," the transformation was complete. The shy gentleman vanished, replaced by a legal warrior who started throwing missiles at anyone within range. It seems the more prominent the hat, the more explosive the rhetoric.

For years, this new, missile-launching Martin stood on a legal high horse, making a mockery of those unfortunate souls caught in the OSP's net who couldn't afford their bail. He famously declared with the confidence of a man who sleeps with a law ledger under his pillow—that as a "senior lawyer," it was practically impossible for him to be detained.

The irony reached its peak when he, a prominent member of ORAL, the team championing the prosecution and arrest of those who allegedly embezzled state funds, found himself being detained. He who called for the shackles of others suddenly felt the cold steel of the law. This happened after a spirited verbal altercation with security at the OSP office, where insults were reportedly traded like currency. After they "bundled" him away, the OSP office requested a landed property in his name as part of his bail, the ultimate "paperwork" punishment for a man who loves his law books.

How else is a lawyer supposed to defend himself if not with his words? Yet, his vocabulary led him straight to a cell. He was then slapped with the very same ridiculous bail conditions that he once ridiculed others for failing to meet. Watching a man who specializes in words being silenced by a demand for a deed was like watching a chef realize he's allergic to his own dish.

While Martin is busy trying to dismantle the office, the Special Prosecutor himself, Kissi Agyebeng, has taken to the airwaves with a surreal defense strategy. He had appeared on TV not just to argue law, but to quote Daddy Lumba's hit tune "Ankwanoma." He stood there like a lonely bird on a podium, singing about his soul and his "time being up" as if we paid the man to mime on a public stage. If we wanted highlife, we could have hired a band for a fraction of the OSP's budget, which, by the way, has apparently spent more money than it has actually collected.

The comedy is compounded by the political pivot of the NDC MPs. Suddenly, the very people who might have cheered for a strong OSP are calling for its head. It seems that when the "Senior Lawyer" of their liking gets caught in the gears, the whole machine must be scrapped.

Martin and his company now claim they possess damning information about the OSP, but he will only share it with the Governing Board. The catch? The Board apparently doesn't exist. It's the perfect legal stalemate: I have the smoking gun, but I'll only show it to the ghost in the room.

So long,

Ato KD

Letter XVI

The Year 2025

Dear Osagyefo,

The year began with the return of John Mahama to the presidency on January 7, promising a national "reset." One of the first things he hit was the "pause" button on the judiciary, suspending Chief Justice Gertrude Torkornoo which eventually led to her dismissal. You’ll be pleased to hear that the Cedi has finally found its rhythm.

After years of falling faster than a ripe mango in a storm, the exchange rate against the Dollar has shown a stabilization that the government describes as a miracle and the rest of us describe as a hostage situation. We have progress, Osagyefo, but it’s the kind of progress where you’re standing still while the rest of the world moves forward in a V8. The most shocking revelation of the season, however, wasn't about the economy, but about the "priorities" of the state.

It turns out that while thousands of our graduates are roaming the streets with degrees, the government allocated over GH₵292 million to procure sanitary pads for high school girls.
